A separate drawing for each size, on the screen's own pixels; the 12px chip on a 2px module. Why: one drawing shrunk to 12 couldn't keep your 2.00px line and still "make sense on their own" (round 1's Seal lost the wheel's spokes, two columns and the cup's handle there).

The corner law

Each law puts the softness in a different place: by joint in the joins, even wear on the outer edge, pressed in the inside corners. Shown on the column front, the conifer and the coupe at 4x, then at real size on the 32 selected pin and the 24 badge, then all 13 at 24.

PlainlyAt 24 on your phone the difference is texture, not shape: you see it at line ends and joins if you look (round ends in even wear, webbed joins in pressed), not at a glance. On the 32 selected pin it shows, and at 4x it's obvious. All three are slight; they differ in kind.

By joint

"personality in the approach to how strokes are connected and curve": Strokes grow into each other: every join is filleted, as if the parts were cast or carved from one piece, while cut ends and tips stay crisp. The curve lives where strokes connect.

The radius follows what the corner is: a join gets a full 1.1px fillet, a shoulder 1.0, the outside of a mass 0.6, the cut end of a line 0.35, a tip or a narrow notch 0.3.

32 selected24

Even wear

"personality in the approach to how strokes are connected and curve": The outline is softened like a rubber stamp or a coin after years of use; strokes still meet in tight, clean joins. The curve lives on the silhouette.

Every outside corner is worn the same 0.38px deep, so the radius follows the angle: a 30° tip 0.2, a right angle 0.92, a 110° apex 1.7. Inside corners barely change (0.2).

32 selected24

Pressed

"personality in the approach to how strokes are connected and curve": Ink pools into every join and notch, so strokes look pressed together, webbed at the joins; outer edges stay sharp. The curve lives inside the corners.

Outside corners 0.3px; inside corners fill 1.5px, as ink pools where two edges meet; a narrow notch 0.6, which still fills deepest because of its angle.

  • By joint. The radius follows what the corner is: a join gets a full 1.1px fillet, a shoulder 1.0, the outside of a mass 0.6, the cut end of a line 0.35, a tip or a narrow notch 0.3. Closest to a carved or engraved block: the joins are shaped, the edges are cut.
  • Even wear. Every outside corner is worn the same 0.38px deep, so the radius follows the angle: a 30° tip 0.2, a right angle 0.92, a 110° apex 1.7. Inside corners barely change (0.2). Closest to a worn stamp or a screen stencil: the outer edge softens, the joins hold.
  • Pressed. Outside corners 0.3px; inside corners fill 1.5px, as ink pools where two edges meet; a narrow notch 0.6, which still fills deepest because of its angle. Closest to letterpress "ink squeeze": ink pushed into the corners of the impression.
  • Why print: ink spreads into inside corners first. Bell Centennial (Matthew Carter, 1975–78) cut ink traps at its joins so phone-book ink could fill them; letterpress printers call the edge build-up "ink squeeze".

The tool

You asked whether another tool or an MCP would be better. We recommend drawing as code, the way these cards were made.

  • The grid is the code. Each glyph is a list of simple shapes. A script joins them into one path with real holes, applies the corner law, and writes the SVG. Change a radius once and all 13 follow; every change is a reviewable line.
  • It works the way your brief reads. "simple shapes, but joined together": the source is the shapes. "minimal bezier curves": each rounded corner is one curve, and we count them. "should not be 100% consistent": the corner law, written once, says how each kind of corner rounds.
  • Figma, via its MCP, is the other route if you want to push curves by hand. Its write tools are in beta, and this environment has no Figma connection.
