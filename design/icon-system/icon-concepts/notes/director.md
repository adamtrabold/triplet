**C1. Optical, not mathematical, centring, with overshoot.**
- The eye balances mass, not the bounding box. Asymmetric glyphs
  (the cup with its handle, the coupe, the tree) need small nudges to
  look centred in the badge, and Apple notes that these adjustments
  are "typically very small, but their effect is noticeable" [1][2].
- Round and pointed forms overshoot flat ones, so they read the same
  size: a circle or a tip goes slightly past the guide a flat edge sits
  on. The amount grows the sharper the form [3][4].
- **Fails:** any icon that reads high or low in a row, or a round or
  pointed icon that reads small beside a flat one.

**C2. Equal optical weight, density AND scale across the set.**
*Amended 2026-10-20 by the owner:* "i also think the scales aren't equal
quite yet -- visually the cut reads as smaller than the others, the
coupe as much taller, and the rest are sort of in the middle".
- **Scale is its own test.** In one row of **our set only** (place icons
  plus the Star and the +), at true size and at 8x, every glyph should
  read the same size (perceived size, not box size). No reference sets
  in this row.
- Tall-narrow glyphs (the coupe) read bigger; low-wide ones (the cup)
  read smaller. Compensate as Apple does, "by scale" [2], plus
  keylines [5].
- **A scale outlier fails C2** even if the blur weight is balanced.
- **Checked first in every round:** the scale row.
- Keylines (circle, square, portrait and landscape rectangle) exist so
  that every icon takes up a similar visual volume whatever its shape.
  They're "a starting point and guide, not a hard rule"; optics win
  [5][6][7].
- Apple balances optically by scale: narrower glyphs grow to match
  [2]. Material separates weight from size and grade so density can be
  tuned without changing size [8].
- **Test:** squint at the whole set in one row. No icon is darker,
  lighter, bigger or smaller.
- **Fails:** any outlier.

**C3. Part proportions.**
- Parts relate to one another the way the object does, optically: a
  rim to a bowl, a stem to a foot, columns to a beam, a handle to a
  cup.
- Parts are simple and repeated sizes recur, but a part grows or
  shrinks optically when it reads too heavy or too thin. Type design
  sizes by eye after measuring [4].
- **Fails:** a part that draws the eye for being too big or too
  slight.

**C4. Counters and gaps that survive the smallest size.**
- Design for the smallest render. Fluent and IBM draw per size for
  this reason, and map symbology sets minimum gaps and widths (see
  `../../grid/notes.md`).
- Every counter and passage is open and meaningful at 12px and at 1x.
  Avoid narrow passages that close up (his cup handle). Never let a
  counter change what the object is (his "pill" columns).
- **Fails:** any gap under the size's minimum, or a counter shape that
  misreads.

**C5. Corner and join treatment suited to each angle.** *Amended
2026-10-20 by the owner:* "that whole thing comes together more when
all points of meeting are at minimum a little bit rounded (and more
rounded in other places".
- His references (examples, not a list): "a signet ring or a
  woodcut/metal cast type piece... an old school banner that has
  screened ink on fabric not necessarily bleeding out, but settling a
  bit less sharp. or even neon signs".
- **Every meeting point has at least a slight round:**
  - inside joins, cut ends, tips, notches and tight counters;
  - square joins and crisp cut ends **no longer pass**;
  - some places are rounder than others (still "not 100% consistent").
- **Still no ink traps or notches** (C6), and **no blobs** (the g7
  "webbed" join). The join round must stay small enough that a join
  doesn't read darker.
- His words: "these *are* very close, so i dont expect or want sweeping
  changes". A round that changes a silhouette, or redraws an icon,
  fails.
- **Interior is less than exterior** (owner, 2026-10-20): "i do think
  tight interior corners should be less rounded than exterior corners
  -- looking at the tree for example, interior is same as exterior
  which i think is wrong."
  - A tight interior corner (notch, counter corner, inside join) gets a
    smaller round than the exterior corners around it, and never zero.
  - Equal interior and exterior rounds fail C5. The tree's notches are
    the test case.
- His "different architecturally sound principles to how different
  angles intersect". In type, acute joins are opened and obtuse ones
  barely touched, and the treatment follows the angle and the role of
  the join [9][10].
- Rounding is sized per intersection: never so broad it blobs a join,
  never so tight it reads sharp. The law is a start, not an excuse.
- **Fails:** any corner he could call "too broad" or "too tight".

**C6. Stroke-contrast compensation at joins.** *Amended 2026-10-19 by
the owner:* "i do not want ink traps... no reason for that on digital
imo".
- Ink traps, notches and V-cuts at joins are **out**.
- Joins must still not read as dark knots at real size, but that has to
  be met without cutting into the join. Allowed means: the join's
  rounding sized small (or none), a part's width or position, or a
  thinner horizontal (as with the 4/3px horizontals).
- Any trap or notch fails C6 outright.
- The type-design citations [9][10] below are history now, not
  guidance for this set.
- (Original text follows.)
- Where parts meet, mass piles up and joins read dark. Type thins
  strokes into junctions and opens ink traps at acute joins, for
  exactly this; Bell Centennial is the classic small-size case
  [9][10].
- **Look for:** dark knots where a stem meets a beam, a handle meets a
  cup, or tiers meet a trunk.
- **Fails:** a visible dark spot at real size.

**C7. Pixel fitting at real render sizes.**
- Mature sets align elements to the pixel grid at their design sizes
  (Material's 24 and 48 grids; IBM's whole-pixel coordinates; Fluent
  per-size drawings) [8][11][12].
- Here: edges land on device pixels at 1x and on 3x dots where the
  1.5px line allows it, and a stated rule says which edges may fall
  between pixels.
- **Fails:** a blurred, uneven edge on a stem or plinth at 1x.

**C8. Consistency of metaphor, perspective AND root shapes.** *Amended
2026-10-20 by the owner:* "how are we determining the root shape of the
drawing? ... the saucer, the base of the coupe, the museum are all at
their core different shapes... a trapezoidal base, a circular base, and
square base respectively. it is adding some visual differentiation we
probably want to align across all icons."
- **Parts that do the same job share one root primitive across the
  set.** Bases, rims and lids, roofs and caps, stems and posts, handles
  and loops are each drawn from the same root shape wherever they
  appear.
- The vocabulary is a grid change, so it's shown as part of the grid,
  not argued per icon.
- **Test:**
  - one row of like parts pulled out of the set (every base side by
    side, every rim, every post);
  - they read as one family of shapes at 8x.
- A like part on a different primitive with no stated reason fails C8.
  A deliberate exception must say why on the board.
- **Scope** is still "no sweeping changes": swap a part's root, don't
  redraw icons.
- One viewpoint (flat front or side elevation), one level of
  literalness, one level of detail across the set. Fluent names icons
  for the object they depict and avoids modifiers that add complexity
  [13].
- **Fails:** one icon in a different perspective, more literal or more
  detailed than its neighbours.

**C9. The grid and the drawing rules express Triplet's brand** (added
2026-10-20, owner: "the grid and various drawing approaches should be
as much a part of the brand as anything").
- The rules themselves (keyshapes, root shapes, corner law, joins,
  weight) carry the brand: printed and hand-marked, souvenir, the
  vintage road-trip brochure.
- They draw on his own references, which are examples, not a list:
  "a signet ring or a woodcut/metal cast type piece... an old school
  banner that has screened ink on fabric... or even neon signs",
  "something screened or pressed".
- **The board says so in plain words:** for each rule, which brand
  quality it carries and why.
- **Fails:**
  - a rule adopted only because another system does it;
  - a rule whose look could belong to any app.
- This is judged against the brand and his words, not against other
  icon sets.

**What I could reach:**
- **Central** (iconists, Martin David and Andreas Storm; commercial):
  - From its page: a 24 grid with a 2px safe area (20 live) and "key
    shapes ... merely guidelines". The fill styles aren't automatic: "This
    is more than just copy, paste and fill ... we carefully review and
    adjust each icon". "All icons are handcrafted."
  - From the page's own example glyphs, rendered large in my scratchpad
    only:
    - Each of 3 weights x 5 corner styles is redrawn, not scaled.
      Proportions and gaps shift with the weight so counters stay open.
    - Inner radii follow outer radii (concentric).
    - The envelope flap's curve lands cleanly in the corners at every
      weight.
    - The round styles use soft, continuous corners, not plain circular
      arcs.
- **Nucleo** (Claudia Romano and Sebastiano Guerriero, since 2014;
  commercial):
  - Its page says little about method ("meticulously crafted").
  - Its homepage glyphs show:
    - Overlaps are cut back with a clean gap: an arrow over a window
      frame, a headset's cable.
    - Every terminal is treated the same way.
    - Small circles are sized optically against the line, not as a
      plain dot.
- **Lucide** (ISC). It publishes a full specification
  (lucide.dev/contribute/icons/specification) and design principles.
  The parts that bear on craft:
  - Corners: radius set by element size and angle, with a stated
    exception for 90° diagonals (1+√2), "even very sharp corners usually
    need a small amount of rounding", and corners stay sharp where more
    than two lines meet.
  - Spacing: at least 2px between elements and inside shapes, tested
    with a 2px circle. "Elements should not terminate with an abrupt cut
    where another element visually continues or intersects them."
  - Visual weight and balance: matched by blurring against a circle and
    a square; asymmetric icons moved off centre to look centred.
  - Density: blur at true size and fix areas that go dark.
  - Curves: "Curves should not contain unintended or abrupt changes in
    curvature"; prefer simple arcs; align adjoining control points for
    continuous curvature; no unneeded points.
  - Shared geometry: shared elements reuse established geometry.
  - Priority: pixel alignment never outranks visual quality.
- **Apple** (HIG, WWDC 2017 "Designing Glyphs"): optical sizing by scale,
  a family's weights drawn as one design, and a designer's eye over the
  maths. Method only, never on a board.

**What it implies for our rubric:**
1. **Curvature continuity becomes a check (new, H-curve).** No abrupt
   change of curvature where a curve meets a line or another curve. Two
   things fail it: a circular arc butted onto a straight, and a fillet
   that kinks. This is the "humanistic curves" half of his v134 note, and
   none of our checks tested it.
2. **The inside-gap test, made ours (G-air).** The live air between parts
   and inside counters gets a stated minimum, tested with a circle of that
   diameter. Ours is 2px of air at 24 (already our rule); now it's
   checked by the circle, not by eye.
3. **Joins where parts cross or meet** (C-joins). Either a clean cut-back
   gap (Nucleo) or a continuous sweep, never an abrupt cut (Lucide).
   Corners where three or more edges meet stay sharp at true size.
4. **Variants are redrawn, not scaled or filled** (Central). Any size or
   weight we ship, such as the 12px chip, the badge or the app icon, is
   checked as its own drawing.
5. **Blur at true size for density and balance** (Lucide, already in our
   weight row). It's now judged at true size in the app, not at 8x.
6. **Visual quality outranks the grid** (Lucide, Apple). The 1/3 lattice
   and the cuts are servants. A grid rule that makes a glyph worse at
